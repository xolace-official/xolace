# Why this year's social life feels different

You come back to a place you know, with people you know, and expect the old rhythm to pick up where it stopped. Then the first few weeks go by and something is off. Fewer people say yes to plans, the usual group is scattered, and a friend you were close to seems like a slightly different person. Almost everyone going into a second, third or later year runs into this, and there are ordinary reasons for it.

## Why the pattern shifts

**There is less time and energy to go round.** Later years often bring more assessed work, deeper material and more riding on the results. Your friends are feeling the same pull. Nobody has stopped caring about the group. They have just got less spare capacity, and so have you.

**People live in different places now.** A friend who used to be one corridor away may now be across town, with a different housemate group and a different commute. Even a short distance changes how often you bump into each other. Running into someone by accident does a lot of the work of keeping a friendship going, and it disappears when you no longer share a building.

**Friendship groups are fluid.** University is a time when people try new subjects, new interests and new ways of being. Some of that pulls people towards each other and some of it pulls them apart. You are probably changing too, often without noticing.

## Why it unsettles you

Most of us lean on habits more than we realise. A regular night out, the table you always sat at, the group chat that was busy by 6pm: these give a week its shape and tell you where you belong in it. When the shape changes, the loss can feel bigger than the thing itself. You might notice it as restlessness, a flat feeling on evenings that used to be full, or a worry that something went wrong between you and the others.

Often nothing did. A disrupted routine feels unsettling in its own right, whatever the reason behind it. Seeing that the discomfort belongs to the change, and not to you, can take some of the sting out.

## Why friends can seem different after a break

A few weeks or months apart is long enough for people to move on from the version of themselves you last saw. They have had a different summer, other people around them, maybe a new look, a new hobby or a new way of talking about things. You have too. The first meeting can feel a bit like talking to someone who is nearly, but not quite, the person you remember.

That feeling usually settles once you spend some ordinary time together again. If it does not, that tells you something useful about where the friendship is now, and it is fine to let it find its own level.

## What helps

**Plan a little more than you used to.** In earlier years, plans could happen by drift. Now it may help to say, "Which evenings are you free this term?" Look at when everyone's workload is lightest, and put one or two things in the diary before the busy stretch starts.

**Find other ways to spend time together.** A late night out is not the only option. A walk, cooking together, a coffee between lectures, studying in the same room, or a weekly film all keep a friendship alive while leaving you with energy for the next day.

**Support each other.** Say out loud that the year feels heavier. Swapping notes on how you each manage your workload, or sitting together to get things done, often brings people closer than the old routine did.

**Value the friendships that still work.** Some will fade a little and some will hold steady. Put your time into the ones that feel easy and mutual, and try not to chase the ones that have run their course.

**It is not too late to make new friends.** People meet others at every stage of university. A club, a society, a volunteering group, a course project or a regular class can all give you a reason to see the same faces each week. Pick one thing that sounds mildly interesting and go a few times before deciding.

> [!TIP]
> Choose one person you would like to see in the first fortnight and send them a specific suggestion, such as "Coffee after Thursday's lecture?" A concrete plan gets a yes much more often than "we should catch up".

## If you feel like you have no one

Feeling lonely at some point is very common, and it often follows a change in your circumstances. The NHS notes that most people feel lonely sometimes, and that it does not have to last. It suggests talking about how you feel with someone you trust, joining a group or class built around something you enjoy, and spending time in places where other people are around.

Your university will almost certainly have a wellbeing or student support service, and it is a good place to start if you feel you have no friends here. They hear this often. They can talk it through with you and point you towards ways of meeting people.

If the loneliness feels heavy, or it has been there for a while, please talk to someone about it. That could be a friend, a family member, a tutor or a counsellor. Asking for support is a sensible step and does not mean anything has gone wrong with you.

A changed social life is not a sign that you did something wrong. It is what a new year does to a group of busy, changing people, and there is plenty of room to build it again.
