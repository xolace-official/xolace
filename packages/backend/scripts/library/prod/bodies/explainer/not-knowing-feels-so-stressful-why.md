# Not knowing feels so stressful, why?

A few weeks before you start university, there is a lot you do not know yet. Who you will live with, whether the course will suit you, whether you will make friends, what an ordinary Tuesday will look like. That can feel oddly heavy, even if you are looking forward to it. There is a reason for that, and some practical ways to carry it more lightly.

## Your brain treats the unknown as a possible threat

Human minds are built to scan for danger. When something is unclear, the sensible default is to stay alert and be ready to react. That is useful if you hear an unfamiliar noise in the house at night. It is less useful when the thing setting off the alarm is an unanswered email from your accommodation office.

Researchers have found that the stress response follows uncertainty quite closely. In one experiment, people learned which images were linked to a mild electric shock. Their reported stress, pupil size and sweat response rose and fell with how unpredictable the shock was, and the unpredictability that could not be resolved by learning more mattered most. In another, people were more anxious and avoided threatening situations more when they could not tell when a threat might arrive, even when the overall risk stayed the same.

## Why a long wait is tiring

Alertness is meant to be temporary. If you are on watch for weeks, with no point at which you get an answer and can stand down, it wears you out. That tiredness often shows up as irritability, poor sleep, going over the same scenarios in your head, or finding it hard to enjoy things you are looking forward to.

Beginning university stacks several unknowns together: a new place, new people, new ways of learning, and often living away from home for the first time. Each is small alone. Together they keep the alert system running.

## Why some uncertainty is fine and some is not

Not every unknown sets off the alarm. A few things tend to make not knowing harder:

- **It matters a lot to you.** The higher the stakes, the louder the alarm.
- **It is open-ended.** Not knowing for a day is easier than not knowing for a month.
- **It feels unanswerable.** Questions you cannot resolve by finding something out are the ones that keep looping.
- **Your own temperament.** Psychologists use the term "intolerance of uncertainty" for a tendency to find not knowing hard to bear and to read unclear situations as threatening. Most of us sit somewhere on that scale.

## What helps

**Sort the unknowns into two piles.** Some questions can be answered or prepared for: where your building is, how to get there, what the first week's timetable looks like, what you need to pack, who to contact if something goes wrong. Make a short list and do those. For the other pile (will I like my flatmates, will it be hard) the honest answer is "I will find out when I am there". Naming that pile as unanswerable for now can stop you trying to solve it at 2am.

**Focus on what you can influence.** The NHS stress advice suggests concentrating on problems you can do something about, and accepting the ones you cannot. Learning a few things about the place, or arranging to meet a course-mate online, is within your control. How others will react to you is not.

**Be careful with reassurance and scrolling.** Searching for certainty, whether that is reading every forum thread, checking social media for strangers' accounts of freshers' week or asking the same question of different people, brings a quick calm that fades, so you do it again. Pick a limited window to look things up, then stop.

**Give worry a slot.** NHS guidance on anxiety describes scheduling a set "worry time", so concerns have somewhere to go that is not every hour of the day. Write the worries down, and leave them until then.

**Keep a routine.** Regular sleep, meals, movement and time with people you like give your day some predictability when much else does not. Keeping the habits you want to take with you is a good way to practise.

**Tell someone.** Saying "I do not know what to expect and it is getting to me" to a friend or family member often takes the edge off.

> [!TIP]
> Write down the three questions that bother you most. Next to each, put either "I can find out" or "I will find out when I am there". Do the first kind this week.

If the worry is constant, is affecting your sleep or eating, or is making it hard to function, it is worth talking to the university counselling centre. You do not need to wait until you arrive to ask.

---

Not knowing is uncomfortable, but it is not a sign you are unprepared, only that you are about to do something new.
