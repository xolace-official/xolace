# For Godwin, my best friend

My name is Emmanuel Acquah, co-founder of Xolace.

Before Xolace became an app, before we started talking about mental health, emotional support, and creating a safe space for people to express themselves, there were experiences in my own life that shaped the way I see these things.

One of those experiences began in 2009.

## Two Kwames, two Mensas

I had just moved to Kasoa and was starting Primary Six in a new school. I didn't know anyone. Then I met Godwin Otabel. He became the very first friend I made in that new school. And somehow, it turned out that we were neighbours too.

From Primary Six, through JHS 1, JHS 2 and JHS 3, Godwin became my closest friend. He wasn't just a classmate. He was my best friend. He was my brother.

There were actually so many things that made our friendship special. We were both called Kwame. And we were both Mensa. In our Akan culture, Mensa refers to the third male-born child. Godwin was the third of three boys, and I was also the third of four boys. So we would joke about being two Mensas and two Kwames.

We just connected.

I have always been someone who enjoys peace and quiet. I'm not really the type who keeps a large circle or talks to everybody. I am careful about the people I allow close to me. So when I say Godwin was my best friend, I mean it.

We shared almost everything. Our families knew each other. I could go to his house, and he could come to mine. During vacations and weekends, we spent so much time together. We went fishing. We went into the bush looking for birds and other creatures. We had adventures that, looking back, were just part of growing up.

We talked about school. We talked about life. We talked about what we wanted to become. We helped each other through school.

And in JHS 3, something else happened that perfectly represented our friendship. I contested for a section prefect position in school and became the prefect. Godwin became my assistant.

It was always us.

## We thought we had time

After JHS, we both went to different Senior High Schools. But distance didn't really change our friendship. Whenever I came home from school, one of the first things I wanted to do was go and see Godwin. Sometimes I would get home, change my clothes and head straight to his house. There were even times I would get there and realise he hadn't returned from school yet. I would still go inside, see his family and wait.

That's how close we were.

After Senior High School, our friendship continued. Godwin became a Jehovah's Witness, and another friend of ours, Godfred, was also a Witness. They would sometimes go for conventions, and I was invited to join them. Even though I wasn't a Witness, I went with them as an invited friend. Those moments around 2017 and early 2018 became some of the memories I now treasure deeply.

We were young. We had finished school. We were trying to figure out life. We spent time together, talked, laughed and made plans.

We thought we had time.

I started working after Senior High School, including teaching in private schools and doing other jobs. Godwin was also working and exploring different things. He was friendly. People loved him. He had his own plans. We had our plans.

## September 26, 2018

That morning, I was getting ready to attend a family naming ceremony with my senior brother. I was dressing up when my phone rang.

It was Godfred. The same Godfred who had gone to school with us. The same Godfred who had also been close to Godwin.

I answered the phone. He was crying. I asked him:

"Godfred, what is it? What has happened?"

And before he even finished telling me, something immediately came into my mind. Godwin. I don't know why. But the moment I heard him crying, I somehow knew that something had happened to Godwin.

Then he told me. Godwin was dead.

I went completely quiet.

I couldn't attend the ceremony anymore. I got dressed and went straight to Godwin's house. When his mother saw me, she started crying. I started crying too. And she said something that I will never forget:

"Kwame, your brother Kwame is here."

That sentence has stayed with me. Because that's exactly what Godwin was. My brother.

There were many people at the house: family members, friends, schoolmates. Everyone was trying to understand what had happened. We were told that Godwin had been taken to the mortuary.

Part of me still couldn't accept it. So I went there with some of the boys. I wanted to see for myself. Maybe there had been a mistake. Maybe something had been misunderstood.

But there was no mistake. The truth was real. Godwin was gone. My best friend was gone. My brother was gone.

And he was so young. We had plans. We had dreams. We had talked about the future. We had talked about continuing our education, working, building our lives and figuring out what came next.

And suddenly, all those plans stopped.

## After

I buried my friend. But in some ways, I also felt like I buried a part of myself.

After Godwin's death, I changed. I became more detached. I became more comfortable keeping things inside. I became careful about getting too attached to people. Because somewhere in my mind was the fear:

"What if I get close to someone again and lose them?"

I had lost someone I deeply loved. And I didn't really have a space to process that loss. I didn't have somewhere I could sit down and say:

"This is what I'm feeling."

"This is what is hurting me."

"This is what I'm going through."

So I dealt with it in my own way. I listened to music. I watched football. I took walks. I spent time alone. I kept things inside. And I tried to continue with life.

But continuing with life and actually healing are not always the same thing.

It took me years to really start moving forward. Even today, there is still a void. There are memories that never completely disappear. And sometimes I still think about the plans we had and wonder what life would have looked like if Godwin were still here.

For a long time, I didn't understand why I had become so emotionally guarded. Why I sometimes kept things to myself. Why expressing certain emotions became difficult. But looking back now, I understand that losing my best friend at such a young age affected me deeply.

And that experience eventually became part of the reason I believe so strongly in having a safe space to express what we feel.

## Why Xolace exists

Years later, I found myself at Koforidua Technical University, studying Computer Science. Eventually, we started building something. Xolace.

At first, it was a startup. But for me, it became something more personal.

Because I know what it feels like to carry something inside you and have nowhere to put it. I know what it feels like to continue going to school, going to work, talking to people and living your normal life while something inside you is hurting. I know what it feels like to need someone to talk to but not know where to start.

And sometimes, people don't necessarily need someone to fix everything. Sometimes they simply need a space. A space to talk. A space to vent. A space to reflect. A space to be heard. A space where they don't have to pretend that everything is okay.

That's part of why we are building Xolace. Because nobody should have to carry everything alone.

Sometimes, one conversation can make a difference. Sometimes, being heard can help someone take the next step. And sometimes, having a place to express what is inside you can be the beginning of healing.

This is my story. This is one of the experiences that shaped me. And this is one of the reasons Xolace exists.

To everyone who has lost someone they love. To everyone carrying something silently. To everyone who has ever felt like they had nowhere to turn.

You don't have to carry it alone.

*Emmanuel Acquah, co-founder, Xolace*
