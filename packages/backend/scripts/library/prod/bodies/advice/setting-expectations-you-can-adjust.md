# Setting expectations you can adjust

Almost everyone imagines university before they get there: the flat, the first lecture, who they'll end up sitting next to. That is normal, and it can be useful. The trouble starts when the picture hardens into a script, either a glowing one or a dreadful one.

## Why a fixed picture backfires

A single, detailed, rosy story is a set-up. Real life will differ from it in dozens of small ways, and each gap can feel like something has gone wrong. In one UK study of first-year students, expectations were largely realistic overall, but the gaps that did show up were about independence: students underestimated how much self-directed study is expected and overestimated how much individual guidance staff can give. That is a reminder that you are probably better calibrated than your nerves suggest.

The all-negative script has its own cost. Dreading it in detail means you spend the weeks before arriving already living through the worst version. It also makes small, ordinary difficulties look like proof.

There is a quieter problem with the glowing version too. In a set of experiments, Heather Kappes and Gabriele Oettingen found that people who indulged in positive fantasies about a hoped-for future showed less energy than people who also thought about what might get in the way. Enjoying the daydream can feel a bit like having already done it.

## What to aim for instead

Oettingen's research group describes a technique called mental contrasting: picture the outcome you want, then picture, specifically, what stands between you and it. Where the goal is realistic, this tends to make people more willing to act; where it isn't, it helps them let go and redirect. You don't need to treat it as a formal method. The takeaway is simply that a picture with some friction in it is more useful than one without.

The aim is a flexible picture that you expect to update, not a prediction you are waiting to be proved right or wrong.

## A ten-minute exercise: three versions of week one

Write a short paragraph for each of these.

1. **The good week.** Something goes well. What actually happened, and what did you do to help it along?
2. **The rough week.** Something goes badly: you get lost, miss a conversation, feel flat on day three. What would you do next, and who could you tell?
3. **The ordinary week.** Mostly unremarkable. A few awkward moments, one decent conversation, some tiredness. This is the most likely one, and it's worth writing in the most detail.

Having all three on paper makes it harder for any one of them to become *the* story. You can also end up with a short list of things to find out.

## Swap guessing for finding out

Imagination fills gaps. Real information is better. It helps to sort your questions by what you are trying to find out, then go to whoever can actually answer.

- **How the teaching works.** This is where expectations and reality tend to part ways. Your course page, timetable and reading list show how much is taught and how much is left to you. A second or third year student or your department can tell you how feedback works and how much you will be expected to organise yourself.
- **What daily life is like.** Someone a year or two ahead of you is your best source: ask what they wish they had known and what turned out easier than expected. Many universities run mentoring or buddy schemes. Photos and videos of the accommodation and campus are useful, though they show the best angles.
- **What to do first.** The university's welcome guide or pre-arrival checklist covers the practical order of things. Online welcome events and open days work in the same way, if you can't get there in person: seeing the actual buildings makes the place less abstract.

> [!TIP]
> Keep a running note called "things to find out". When something worries you, turn it into a question you can actually ask.

## Don't measure yourself against other people's version

Other people's pre-arrival stories, and the polished ones online, are scripts too. The Mental Health Foundation advises not comparing your social life with others'; some people want a lot of nights out and others prefer a quiet evening in, and neither makes you less of a student.

If the dread is getting in the way of sleeping, eating or getting on with your day, it is worth talking to someone: the university counselling centre, or someone you trust. The NHS notes that anxiety is a normal feeling that is worth getting support for once it starts affecting daily life.

---

Hope for a good start, plan for an ordinary one, and leave room to be surprised.
