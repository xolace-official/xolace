# Decoding the place: the words and the people

Every university runs on its own shorthand, and on a set of relationships nobody explains in advance. Both are learnable. Neither is a test of whether you belong.

## Why the language feels like a wall

Researchers who study higher education talk about a "hidden curriculum": the things that matter for getting on, but that nobody teaches. One account from the University of Salford splits it into two parts. One is belonging, whether you feel you fit in. The other is the "rules of the game": the processes, terms and expectations that insiders pick up without noticing. Jargon sits where the two meet. If you don't know what a word means, it is easy to read that as a sign you're an outsider, when it only means nobody has told you yet.

This tends to land hardest on students who can't lean on someone who has been through it, such as first-generation students, mature students and those from backgrounds that universities have historically under-served. The same paper argues that spelling things out before arrival helps with belonging and with staying on the course. So looking things up isn't cheating. It's the point.

## A starter glossary

Most of these are UK terms, and some change from country to country, so here they are in plain English:

- **Lecture:** a large-group session, often in a hall, where you mostly listen and take notes.
- **Seminar:** a smaller class, discussion led by an academic, usually with some preparation beforehand.
- **Tutorial:** a one-to-one or very small group session, more interactive still.
- **Module:** one unit of study within your course, in the US usually called a "class" or "course".
- **Formative and summative assessment:** formative work gets you feedback but doesn't count towards your grade; summative work does.
- **Personal tutor:** an academic who keeps an eye on how you're getting on overall.
- **Students' Union:** the students' organisation that runs clubs and societies and often an advice service. It is separate from the university itself.
- **Student Representative Council (SRC):** the elected student body that speaks for students to the university. In some places it plays the role the Students' Union plays elsewhere.

## Same thing, different name

Names for the same thing differ from one university to the next. Some places call the first week "Freshers'", others "Welcome". Even the calendar varies: Salford, for instance, divides the year into three trimesters of about twelve weeks, where many places use two semesters. When a name doesn't ring a bell, ask what it covers instead of assuming you've missed something.

Every subject also has words that sound ordinary but mean something narrower inside it. Expect a few to confuse you at first. That's normal, and it passes with use.

## What to do with a word you don't know

- **Ask, early and plainly.** "Sorry, what does that stand for?" is one of the most ordinary sentences in a first-year seminar, and others are usually glad someone said it.
- **Keep a running list.** A note on your phone is enough. Add the word, the meaning, and where you heard it. Within a few weeks the list stops growing.
- **Read ahead.** Your university's website and any glossary it publishes, and student forums if you use them, will cover a lot before you arrive.

> [!TIP]
> When a term is explained to you, write it down straight away. You'll be surprised how much you retain.

## Academic staff: less structured than school

At school, the relationship with teachers is fairly fixed. At university it is looser, and it varies with how big your class is, what discipline you're in, and what the person's role involves. A lecturer in a hall of hundreds will take longer to learn your name than one in a lab of ten. Some academics are mainly researchers and may be harder to catch. You are also expected to take more of the initiative.

Rather than guess, ask, and make the message easy to answer. Something like:

> Hi Dr [Surname], I'm a first-year on [course]. What's the best way to reach you, and roughly how long should I expect a reply to take?

For how to address someone, follow how they introduce themselves or sign off: a first name if they use theirs, a title if they use one. If you're still unsure, a title is a safe start. Don't read a slow reply as a brush-off. Staff are juggling a lot of students.

## Professional staff: the people who run the rest

Beyond teaching, a university employs many people whose job is to help you. Names and set-ups differ, but here is what they are for:

- **To learn:** librarians, often with a named subject specialist who can help you find and use sources, and academic skills services, who teach study techniques, writing and referencing.
- **To get settled:** accommodation and student services teams, who handle housing, fees and general queries (many universities route these through a single hub), and the Students' Union advice centre, which at some universities offers free, confidential help.
- **To get through something hard:** wellbeing and counselling teams, and disability and inclusion teams, who arrange adjustments for disabilities, long-term health conditions and learning differences such as dyslexia. At Newcastle, for example, you don't need a formal diagnosis to start the conversation.
- **To plan ahead:** careers and employability advisers, for CVs, applications and planning, and not only in final year.

Most of these can be used before things go wrong, and some need registering with in advance, so it's worth finding out now which ones your university has.

If the worry about not understanding, or not fitting in, starts to weigh on you day to day, it's worth talking to your university counselling centre, or someone you trust.

---

Not knowing the words yet says nothing about you, and the answers are usually one question away.
