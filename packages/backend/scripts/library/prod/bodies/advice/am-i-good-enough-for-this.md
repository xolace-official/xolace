# "Am I good enough for this?"

Most people about to start university have asked themselves some version of this question. It tends to get louder in the weeks before term, when there is little to do except imagine the hardest version of what is coming. If that sounds familiar, you are in very good company. This piece looks at where the doubt comes from, why it is usually a poor guide to your actual ability, and what you can do about it before you arrive.

## What the doubt actually is

Psychologists have a name for your belief in your own ability to handle a particular kind of task: self-efficacy, an idea developed by Albert Bandura. It is not the same as ability. It is your *estimate* of your ability, and that estimate can be badly out of step with the facts, particularly when you are about to do something new.

Its strongest source, according to Bandura's theory, is having done something hard before. That is the catch with a first year. You have no track record yet at this level, so the estimate has nothing recent to lean on, and worry fills the gap. That is how the estimate works, not a flaw in you.

A related experience is often called impostor syndrome or the impostor phenomenon: feeling that you got in by luck, and that someone will soon notice. It is not a diagnosis. A systematic review of 62 studies and over 14,000 people found estimates ranging from 9% to 82%, depending largely on the questionnaire and cut-off used, so the exact figure is uncertain. What it does show is that these feelings are common, and that they often sit alongside anxiety and low mood. If you recognise yourself in this, you are far from alone.

## Why having a place counts as evidence

Universities do not hand out places at random. You were assessed on your grades, your work and, often, a personal statement or interview, by people whose job is to judge whether you can cope with the course. You earned that place. That is evidence, and it is better evidence than a feeling at 2am.

A useful habit, borrowed from the NHS's self-help material on unhelpful thoughts, is to "check" a worry rather than argue with it: what is the evidence for it, and what is the evidence against? "I'll be found out" has very little going for it. "I got through years of exams and coursework to be here" has a lot.

## Why avoiding it feels natural, and why it backfires

When something feels beyond you, the instinct is to stay away from it: no looking at the reading list, no thinking about the course. It brings relief for an evening. But if having done something is the strongest source of confidence, avoiding it means skipping the very experiences that would show you can. The doubt stays untested, so it stays.

## Small ways to test it

You do not need to study in advance. Bandura's theory names four places a sense of ability comes from, and each can be given a small, low-pressure push before term starts. The aim is to meet the subject in small doses so that it stops being an unknown.

- **Do a little of it.** Doing is the strongest source, so start here. Pick one item from the reading list, ideally the shortest, and read a chapter. Notice how much you can follow. What you can't follow is a question to ask, not a verdict.
- **See someone like you do it.** Watching people cope builds belief too, especially people close to your own position. Many universities and reputable educational providers put introductory lectures online for free. Treat one as a taster, not homework.
- **Ask someone who has seen your work.** Encouragement counts most when it comes from someone who knows what you can do. A teacher, tutor or friend can tell you what they see in your work, which gives you something specific to hold on to.
- **Look after the baseline.** Tiredness and nerves are easy to read as signs that you can't cope. Sleep, meals and breaks don't change your ability, but they change how the doubt feels.

> [!TIP]
> Keep a short note of what you've managed this year: a mark you were pleased with, feedback that stuck, something you worked out the hard way. Add a line on what drew you to the subject. You will want both in the first month of term.

## The help that is built in

Universities expect first years to need support, and they staff for it. In some countries, a *personal tutor* is an academic assigned to check in on you and point you to the right help. *Librarians* help with finding and using sources, and many run workshops. *Academic skills* teams offer free sessions on essay writing, note-taking and referencing, and some of these are one-to-one. Do find what your university can offer. Using them is routine. One university skills service even tells students they can come back for more sessions as often as they book.

Outside the UK the names differ, but most universities have a learning centre, tutoring service or academic advising office.

If the doubt starts to stop you sleeping, eating normally or enjoying things, it's worth talking to the university counselling centre or someone you trust. Asking early is fine, and you don't need to wait until it gets worse.

---

Feeling unready is a normal part of stepping up, and it says very little about whether you will manage. You have more to go on than it feels like right now.
