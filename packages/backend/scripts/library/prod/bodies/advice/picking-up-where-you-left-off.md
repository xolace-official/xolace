# Picking up where you left off

Going back to a university you already know is a different feeling from arriving for the first time. You know where things are, but you also know what the place can ask of you, and a break can make that memory sharper or fuzzier. No two years are quite alike. The days before term are a good moment to take what you learned last time and use it, without turning it into a big project.

## Take a short look back

Set aside twenty minutes with a pen or a notes app and go through last year, one area at a time: your course, where you lived, how you spent your time, how you looked after yourself. For each, jot down what worked, what didn't, and what you now know that you didn't then.

- **What worked.** The study spot that kept you focused, the week you got ahead, the weekly call home. These are worth repeating on purpose, since good things are easy to forget once life speeds up.
- **What didn't.** Late-night deadlines, skipping meals when busy, a module you left too long. Name them plainly and without blame. It is information, not a verdict.
- **What you learned.** You know how the system works, who to email, how your own energy rises and falls. That counts for a lot, even if last year felt rough.

Then pick one or two changes you could realistically make. A short list you can follow beats a long one you'll abandon by week three. If last year was hard, this look back can stir up some of that feeling. Stop when you've had enough, and think about talking it through with someone you trust.

## Ease back into your subject

After a break, the first lectures can feel like landing in the middle of a conversation. Reconnecting a little beforehand softens that, and it doesn't take much.

- **Skim your old notes.** This helps most when the new term builds on the last one. Try to recall the main ideas before you look, then read back over what you learned, so the foundations are fresh when the next layer arrives.
- **Look ahead lightly.** If the reading list or module outline is out, read one chapter or the introduction to something. You are getting the shape of it, and you don't need to understand it all.
- **Listen or watch.** A podcast, a talk or a documentary connected to your subject counts, and you can do it on a walk or while cooking.

Half an hour on a few days is plenty.

> [!TIP]
> Stop while it still feels easy. Ending a session when you could do a bit more makes it more likely you'll come back to it tomorrow.

## Use the start of term as a reset point

New terms, like birthdays or the first of a month, work as natural markers. Researchers who studied this "fresh start effect" found that people are more likely to take up goals just after moments like these, including the start of a semester, perhaps because they feel like the line between an old chapter and a new one. Your routine is changing anyway, so a new habit has less to fight against.

The trick is to choose one habit, not five. Something small, tied to something you already do:

- a ten-minute walk after your first class
- a glass of water and a proper breakfast before you open your laptop
- a regular wind-down time on weeknights
- one evening a week with no studying

Work out the practical side in advance. Pack the trainers, put the alarm in your phone, or ask a friend to join you. Habits take different lengths of time to settle. In one study following people building everyday habits, the time it took ranged from a few weeks to several months, and missing a single day did not undo the progress. So if you slip in week two, carry on. You haven't failed.

## Sort the practical things early

Money and housing are the two things most likely to turn a good first fortnight into a stressful one, and both are easier to deal with before term starts.

**Money**

- **Expect gaps.** Student funding, wages from summer work or family support may arrive after term begins. Work out roughly what you'll need for the first few weeks, covering rent, food and travel, and what you have to cover it.
- **Be honest with friends.** If cash is tight, it's fine to skip some of the early nights out or to suggest cheaper plans.
- **Ask for help sooner.** Most universities have a financial support or hardship service. Contacting them before things reach a crisis is usually easier for everyone, and asking is what the service is for.

**Housing and bills**

- **Know where you're living.** If it isn't settled, make it your first job, because not knowing is a draining way to start. Check the tenancy dates, deposit and moving-in arrangements.
- **Agree the household basics.** If you share, talk with housemates before term about how bills are split and paid, who is in charge of what and how you'll handle disagreements. Writing it down helps. Awkward conversations are much easier before there's a problem.

If friendships are on your mind, they get their own entry: "Why this year's social life feels different".

## If it still feels heavy

Mixed feelings about going back are common, even if you liked it last year. If dread is affecting your sleep, appetite or ability to function day to day, speak to your doctor or the university's counselling service. You don't need to wait until it gets worse.

---

You already know more than you did a year ago. Use a little of it now, pick one small thing to start, and let the rest take shape as term does.
