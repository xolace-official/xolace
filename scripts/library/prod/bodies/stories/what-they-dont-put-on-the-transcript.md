# What they don't put on the transcript

My transcript shows my grades. It doesn't show the day I sat in an office during my industrial attachment and cried.

This is the story behind that day, and what it taught me. I hope it helps someone going through something similar.

## Level 100: broke and quiet

I came to Koforidua in 2022 with a substantial amount of money, enough to cover my expenses for a whole academic year, which was about nine months. It was my third time there. The first was for the NCCE E-Quiz when I was in SHS. The second was after SHS, when I came to find my feet. The third was for university.

But the truth was, I barely knew the place.

I loved the freedom that came with university life. Maybe a little too much. Before I knew it, the money was gone in not even two months.

I didn't tell anyone. I kept quiet and carried it alone.

Food was never my problem. My roommate, Richard Tetteh Martey, made sure of that. He was a chef, too. Four years together, and we never had a single argument.

But I still wasn't okay. I got attached to a girl, got hurt badly, and found it difficult to concentrate on my studies. My Level 100 results showed it.

## Level 200: alone, but coding

I moved to a new hostel and decided that being alone was better. I was both right and wrong.

Right, because that was when I fell in love with coding. Fixing a bug felt amazing, and I would celebrate with a chocolate or a yoghurt. A senior who coded also inspired me.

Wrong, because I still wasn't telling anyone what was going on in my head.

## The day I cried

At the end of Level 200, I went for my industrial attachment. I could code a little, but I felt I couldn't offer the company much. I felt useless, and it seemed as though no one cared.

I sat down and cried.

Then I asked myself what I was even doing at university. The answer was simple: it was better late than never. I started taking programming seriously. No day without coding or learning. No more tutorial hell. I learned from real developers and started building things myself.

## The turn

I was also studying online at the University of the People at the time. That experience taught me something that stayed with me: asking for help is not a weakness; it is a strength.

So, during a break in Level 300, I messaged Deen, my boss man, and more of a friend than anything. I told him I felt stuck and asked which courses or books he would recommend.

He did more than answer. He brought me onto a real project. At first, I was learning. A month later, I was getting paid. My first developer pay!

Deen treated me like a younger brother. He gave me advice, opportunities, and connections. He also brought me onto a project with Nathaniel, who is now our CEO at Xolace. I was already working on the project before Nathaniel joined me. It's funny how life works. Today, we're building together, and he's leading us at Xolace. My geography teacher was right after all: the earth really is round.

When the Xolace idea was shared with me, I saw that it was solving a problem I had experienced myself. All those years, I wasn't lonely because I refused to reach out. I was lonely because I couldn't find the words for what I was feeling.

## Start at the edge

There's a line I love, often credited to the FBI: "The way to conquer the center is first to conquer the edge."

Here's how I understand it. When something heavy sits in the middle of your life, you don't have to face it all at once. You can start at the edge. Find the first small word for what you feel. That one step can make the center easier to face. This idea is part of what inspired Xolace.

> Sometimes, the hardest part is admitting you need to grow.

## Four lessons

**1. Silence costs more than money.** I lost the money in less than two months. I carried the silence for years. Gilbert's Law puts it this way: "Most conflict is caused by unspoken problems. So, problem solve by speaking up."

Research suggests that putting feelings into words can help regulate emotional responses. Psychologist Matthew Lieberman's research has explored how affect labelling, putting feelings into words, can reduce activity in parts of the brain involved in processing threats. Psychiatrist Dan Siegel sums up the idea with the phrase, "Name it to tame it."

**2. Feeling useless is information, not a verdict.** That industrial attachment experience hurt, but it also showed me what I needed to work on. I had to be honest with myself, put in the work, and keep learning. During my days at ALX, one message that stuck with me was simple: "We do hard things." And sometimes, doing hard things starts with accepting where you are and deciding to get better.

**3. Asking for help is a skill.** One honest message to Deen changed the direction of my career.

**4. Use the gaps.** In his essay *How Universities Should Prepare Founders*, Paul Graham discusses the potential of the gap between the end of classes and the start of exams: the reading period. Students are meant to use that time to study, but with people still on campus and regular lectures paused, it can also create space to explore ideas and start building something. My turn came in a similar kind of gap, during school breaks, when I finally had room to learn.

## One thing to try

Think of one person who is a step ahead of you. Send them one clear question today. Not just "Please help me," but "I'm stuck on this. What would you read or try?"

## Ask yourself

- What am I carrying that I haven't said out loud?
- Who is one step ahead of me whom I can ask?
- What can I start in the gap I have this week?

If what you're carrying feels too heavy, talk to someone you trust or a mental health professional. You don't have to carry it alone. And if you can't find the words yet, that is exactly why we built Xolace.
